ANCOVA

Analysis of covariance tests whether groups differ in a variable once one or two continuous covariates are held constant — whether treated animals eat less than controls for their body weight, or burn more energy for their lean and fat mass. It combines an ANOVA with a regression on the covariates, and follows the procedure of Speakman, Fletcher & Vaanholt (2013), The '39 steps': an algorithm for performing statistical analysis of data on energy intake and expenditure (Disease Models & Mechanisms 6:293–301): it first tests whether the covariate's slope differs between the groups, drops that test when it is not significant, and reports which step of the paper's algorithm the result corresponds to.

Settings

  • Dependent variable — the variable to test, such as energy expenditure or food intake. Do not divide it by body mass first: the analysis corrects for body size itself.
  • Covariates — One covariate (body mass, say) or Two covariates (e.g. lean and fat mass).
  • Covariate and Second covariate — the continuous variables to adjust for; they must differ from each other and from the dependent variable. An animal property such as the weight becomes a variable through Add Variable.
  • Factor — the between-subject factor (genotype, treatment).
  • Slopes — whether a covariate's slope may differ between the groups:
    • Test, drop if not significant (the paper's procedure) — the model starts with every group × covariate interaction (with two covariates also their interaction) and drops the non-significant ones, the least significant first.
    • Always include — keeps every interaction.
    • Assume parallel — the classic ANCOVA, without interactions.
  • Alpha — the significance level of every decision (0.05, 0.1 or 0.01).
  • P-value adjustment — for the pairwise comparisons: Tukey-Kramer (the default) or one of the corrections of the other ANOVAs.

The analysis uses one value per animal: each variable aggregated over the animal's rows with its aggregation. For animals measured under several conditions, use the Linear Mixed Model — the paper's "individual as a random factor".

Result

  • Outcome — one paragraph first: which effects are significant and the step of the paper that applies — for example step 26 (the groups differ independently of body mass), step 25 (the difference is due to body size), step 28 (both matter, the slopes are parallel), step 30 (the slopes differ) or, with two covariates, steps 33–36. It also names the interactions that were dropped.
  • Covariates by group — whether the groups differ in each covariate (one-way ANOVA) and its mean per group.
  • Full model (type III) — every term with SS, DF, MS, F, p_unc and np2 (partial eta-square), before any interaction is dropped. The tests are type III on sum-to-zero coded factors, as SPSS, Minitab and R compute them.
  • Final model (type III) — the same columns after the non-significant interactions are dropped, with R² and adjusted R². While a group × covariate interaction stays in the model, the factor's row tests the groups where the covariate is 0; compare the groups with the adjusted means and the Johnson–Neyman ranges instead.
  • Slopes — the change in the dependent variable per unit of each covariate, per group when its slope differs.
  • Adjusted means — each group's mean predicted at the covariates' overall means, with its standard error and 95 % interval, next to the raw mean.
  • Pairwise comparisons of the adjusted means — for each pair of groups A and B: diff (A − B), se, T, p_unc, the adjusted p-value (p_tukey, or p_corr) and d (the difference over the residual standard deviation).
  • Johnson–Neyman regions of significance — with one covariate whose slope differs between the groups: the covariate values at which two groups differ significantly.
  • Model checks — Shapiro–Wilk of the residuals and the Brown–Forsythe test of their variances across the groups. With two covariates, their correlation and variance inflation factor: the analysis assumes independent covariates.
  • Sensitivity — when the factor is not significant: the smallest group effect this design would detect with 80 % power (step 11 of the paper).
  • The figure — the data against each covariate with the final model's fitted line per group; diamonds mark the adjusted means. With two covariates, one panel per covariate, the points adjusted to the other covariate's mean.

In a pipeline, an ANCOVA node also publishes its result as numbers — p_factor, p_covariate, p_interaction, interaction_kept, step and more — that a Decision node routes on; Example: the 39 steps builds the paper's procedure around it.

Example interpretation

The paper's own example: the metabolism of 15 animals in three treatment levels, with body mass as the covariate.

  1. Slopes. The full model's Levels × Body mass interaction has p = 0.31: the slopes do not differ, so it is dropped.
  2. Final model. Levels is significant (F = 45.96, p < 0.001); body mass only shows a trend (p = 0.063).
  3. Outcome. Step 26: the treatment levels differ in metabolism independently of body mass. The pairwise comparisons of the adjusted means show that every level differs from the others.
